# The Strategy Map: Direction × Volatility × Time

## @hook
Options strategies go by dozens of names, but each one answers just three questions: **Which way will the stock go? Will it move more or less than the market expects? Over what period?** Find your cell on the map first, then pick a structure inside it — not the other way round. This lesson puts every strategy from [[long-options]] to [[vertical-spreads]], plus the volatility strategies still to come, onto one map.

## @bridge
In this stage we met long options ([[long-options]]), covered calls ([[covered-call]]), cash-secured puts ([[cash-secured-put]]), protective puts and collars ([[protective-put-collar]]) and vertical spreads ([[vertical-spreads]]). All of them are built from the bricks of [[payoff-lego]], and all can be described by the four Greeks of [[greeks-map]]. This lesson is both a summary and a turning point: it puts the two dimensions beyond direction — volatility and time — on the table, paving the way for the straddles, condors and calendars of the next stage. It builds Idea ① (shape), Idea ③ (volatility) and Idea ④ (risk).

## @intuition
Back to Kai's three wishes.

> [!KAI] Three wishes, three signatures
> - **protect the shares** → protective put: Δ falls from 100 shares to 84, and Kai **buys** volatility (Γ and ν positive);
> - **earn rent while waiting** → covered call: Δ falls to 78 shares, and Kai **sells** volatility (Γ and ν negative);
> - **bet on a big move with a capped loss** → long call: Δ positive, **buying** volatility.

All three are variations on owning XYZ, yet the biggest difference between them isn't direction but **volatility**: one buys insurance, the other sells it. That is the map's second dimension.

A complete view splits into three independent questions:

1. **Direction:** bullish, neutral or bearish? → expressed by **Δ**;
2. **Volatility:** will the stock actually move **more** or **less** than the volatility implied in option prices? → expressed by **Γ and ν**, with Θ as Γ's other face;
3. **Time:** over what period does the view hold? Is the view on the near term the same as on the far term? → expressed by the **tenor** and by **time spreads**.

One trick for reading the map: **the sign of Δ is set only by the column; the signs of Γ and ν only by the row.** All three bullish cells have positive Δ, but the first row has positive ν and the third negative. So “I'm bullish” only picks a column; to pick a cell you must also answer “do I think the volatility the market is charging is too expensive or too cheap?”

> [!THINK] Two of Kai's friends are both bullish on XYZ for the next 30 days. One buys the 100 call for 2.45; the other sells the 95 put for 0.51. At expiry XYZ has drifted up to 101. Who did better? What if it had jumped to 110 instead?
> They chose the same column. What else did each of them choose?
> ---
> At 101 the call is worth 1, a 1.45 loss; the put expires and its seller keeps 0.51. At 110 the call is worth 10, a 7.55 gain, while the put seller still keeps only 0.51. Same direction, opposite rows: the call buyer bet that XYZ would move **more** than option prices implied, the put seller that it would move **less**. A small drift rewards the seller; a big move rewards the buyer.

We'll take it in five parts:

- **① The signature table: every strategy's Δ, Γ, Θ and ν**
- **② The volatility dimension: betting on realized above or below implied**
- **③ The time dimension: short-dated means Γ and Θ, long-dated means ν**
- **④ A selection process: view → cell → structure → size**
- **⑤ Common mistakes: mismatched dimensions**

The table obeys one rule with almost no exceptions: **Γ and Θ have opposite signs.** The reason is the Black-Scholes equation ([[black-scholes]]). Ignoring interest:

$$
\Theta \approx -\tfrac12\,\sigma^2 S^2\,\Gamma
$$

where \(\Theta\) is per year, \(\sigma\) the implied volatility, \(S\) the stock price and \(\Gamma\) the position's gamma. If you want convexity (Γ positive) you pay for it with time every day (Θ negative); if you want to collect time every day, you must sell convexity.

> [!EXAMPLE] The 30-day at-the-money call
> \(\Gamma = 0.0693\), \(\sigma = 0.20\), \(S = 100\):
> $$
> \begin{gathered}\Theta \approx -\tfrac12 \times 0.20^2 \times 100^2 \times 0.0693 = -13.86 \text{ per year} \\ \Rightarrow\ -13.86 / 365 \approx -0.038 \text{ per day}\end{gathered}
> $$
> The exact value is −0.044; the extra comes from the interest term. The one exception is the time spread: a calendar sells near-dated Γ and buys far-dated ν, so Γ is negative while ν is positive — **Γ and ν of opposite sign are the fingerprint of a time spread.**

### ② The volatility dimension: betting on realized above or below implied

A long-gamma position pays theta every day and earns it back from moves: a daily move of \(\Delta S\) brings in roughly \(\tfrac12\Gamma(\Delta S)^2\). Set the two equal and you get the **breakeven daily move**:

$$
\tfrac12\,\Gamma\,(\Delta S^{*})^2 = |\Theta_{\text{day}}| \quad\Rightarrow\quad \Delta S^{*} \approx \frac{S\sigma}{\sqrt{365}}
$$

where \(\Theta_{\text{day}}\) is the daily theta and \(\sigma\) the implied volatility at which you bought.

> [!EXAMPLE] XYZ's breakeven move
> \(\Delta S^{*} \approx 100 \times 0.20 / \sqrt{365} \approx 1.05\) dollars (ignoring interest; with the exact Θ and Γ at a 4% rate it is about 1.12). If XYZ moves more than about $1.05 a day on average, the long-gamma position makes money; if less, the short-gamma position does. **“Long volatility” precisely means betting that realized volatility will exceed the implied volatility you bought at** ([[theta]], [[delta-hedging]]).

The same logic works over a month: the 30-day at-the-money straddle costs 4.57, while a one-standard-deviation 30-day move is \(S\sigma\sqrt{T} = 5.73\), so the straddle costs about 0.8 times that ([[straddle-strangle]]). The straddle buyer bets XYZ will move more than that price implies. On average, index implied volatility has exceeded the realized volatility that followed, which gives the third row (selling volatility) a long-run premium — and makes it lose in concentrated bursts during crashes ([[variance-risk-premium]]).

So the tenor is itself a view: **if the view is “a big move is coming soon,” go short-dated; if it is “implied volatility will rise across the board,” go long-dated.** If your views on the near and far terms differ — say, calm in the near term but far-dated implied volatility too low — you are in the middle row: calendars and diagonals that sell near-dated and buy far-dated options ([[calendar-diagonal]]).

Experienced traders often add a fourth question: **which strikes are expensive?** Real option chains are not priced at one flat volatility; out-of-the-money puts usually carry higher implied volatility than calls (the skew, [[smile-skew]]). The same cell can then be filled in a cheaper or a dearer way: a bearish view bought through puts pays the skew, while selling puts collects it. Structures such as risk reversals and ratio spreads exist precisely to trade that fourth dimension ([[ratios-risk-reversals]]). The map in this lesson is drawn at one flat 20% volatility so that the first three dimensions stand out clearly.

### ④ A selection process: view → cell → structure → size

A process you can reuse (a method, not advice):

1. **Write down the view's three dimensions:** direction, volatility (is implied volatility above or below the realized volatility you expect?), and time.
2. **Find the cell on the map:** for example “mildly bullish + implied volatility looks rich + 30 days” → bullish × short vol.
3. **Pick a structure within the cell:** inside one cell, structures line up by whether the loss is capped and by reward-to-risk. Bullish × short vol could be a cash-secured put (big loss, high win rate), a bull put spread (capped loss) or a covered call (if you already own the stock).
4. **Size by maximum loss** ([[position-sizing]]).

> [!EXAMPLE] A $300 risk budget
> Kai is willing to lose at most $300 on a bullish view:
> - long 30-day 100 call: maximum loss $245 → 1 contract;
> - 100/105 bull call spread: maximum loss $174 → 1 spread (2 would exceed the budget);
> - 95/100 bull put spread: maximum loss $339 → none; it is over budget;
> - cash-secured 95 put: maximum loss $9,449 → not even in the conversation for this budget.
>
> For the same directional view, the risk budget has already ruled out half the structures.

Here is the whole process applied to Kai:

1. **View:** XYZ rises gently over 30 days, to about 105 and probably not much further; earnings are three weeks away, and 20% implied volatility looks neither cheap nor expensive.
2. **Cell:** bullish × no strong volatility view → the structure in the bullish column with the least ν.
3. **Structure:** the 100/105 bull call spread — the sold leg sits right on the target, and its ν is only a quarter of a single call's, so implied volatility moving around earnings matters little.
4. **Size:** one spread, maximum loss $174, inside the $300 budget.
5. **Plan:** write down when to leave — at some share of the maximum profit, if XYZ falls below 97 (the view is invalidated), or with two weeks left for a fresh look ([[first-trade]]).

With the same direction but a belief that earnings will bring a far bigger move than priced, the cell becomes bullish × long vol and the structure a single long call, accepting its larger Θ and ν. **The structure changes with every dimension of the view, not only with direction.**

### ⑤ Common mistakes: mismatched dimensions

- **Expressing a volatility view with a directional trade:** the example from [[vertical-spreads]] — before earnings a call is bought at 40% implied volatility for 4.73; the next day XYZ is at 103, implied volatility is back to 20%, and the call is worth 4.31, a 0.42 loss despite the right direction, while the 100/105 bull call spread gains 0.70. If your view is only about direction, use a structure with little ν (a spread); if your view is “it will move a lot,” trade volatility directly (a straddle) instead of betting on it “on the side” with a one-way option ([[earnings-events]]).
- **Treating “income” as having no view:** covered calls, short puts and iron condors all live in the third row. They aren't “neutral income”; they express a definite view: realized volatility will come in below implied.
- **Thinking delta-neutral means riskless:** the straddle has a Δ of only +6.9 shares but the largest Γ and ν of any structure in the table. Delta-neutral only means you aren't betting on direction, not that you carry no risk ([[portfolio-risk]]).
- **Doubling up across positions:** Kai holding both a covered call and a cash-secured put looks like two “income strategies,” but it is two helpings of bullish × short vol — the Δ and the negative Γ stack up. Only by adding up the Greeks of every position can you see the true exposure.
- **Forgetting that every leg costs a spread:** a four-leg iron condor crosses four bid-ask spreads to open and four more to close. On a structure whose maximum profit is 1.02 per share, a few cents per leg is a large share of the edge ([[liquidity-spreads]]).

> [!KEY] In one sentence
> Answer the three questions (direction, volatility, time) first, then choose a structure. A structure's name doesn't matter; its signature does: **Δ for direction, Γ and ν for whether you're buying or selling volatility, and the tenor for whether you're betting on the next few days or on implied volatility itself.**

## @analogy
Choosing a strategy is like **packing for a trip**.

- **Direction** is the destination's climate: the tropics or the mountains decide whether you pack T-shirts or a down jacket.
- **Volatility** is how changeable the weather will be: if the forecast says “changeable,” you pack several layers (buy options — pay for the ability to adapt); if you're sure the weather will be steady, you pack one outfit and rent the spare suitcase space to someone else (sell options — get paid).
- **Time** is the length of the trip: a weekend away and a three-month stay call for handling very different amounts of change.

A strategy's name is like a clothing brand; its signature is what the garment actually does: warmth (Δ), adjustability (Γ), and how much suitcase space it takes up (Θ).

The analogy differs in one important way: on a trip you are up against the weather itself, while in the options market you are up against **the price of the weather forecast**. The market has already priced “how changeable it will be” into option prices (implied volatility). You make money not when the weather is changeable, but when it is more changeable than forecast — or steadier.

## @misconceptions
- **“If I'm bullish, I buy calls.”** — Being bullish only picks a column. That column also holds short-volatility structures (short puts, covered calls), which may fit the view better when implied volatility looks rich.
- **“Income strategies carry no direction or volatility risk.”** — Covered calls, short puts and iron condors all sell volatility; their view is that realized volatility will fall short of implied.
- **“A delta-neutral position is riskless.”** — A long straddle's Δ is nearly zero while its Γ and ν are the largest in the table; a short straddle's Δ is nearly zero too, and its loss can be unlimited.
- **“Structures in the same cell are more or less the same.”** — A cell only fixes the signs of the Greeks. In the same cell, the cash-secured put can lose $9,449 and the bull put spread $339.
- **“The best position has positive Γ and positive Θ.”** — Within a single expiry it essentially doesn't exist: \(\Theta \approx -\tfrac12\sigma^2S^2\Gamma\), so convexity must be paid for with time.

## @takeaways
- Every strategy answers three questions: direction (Δ), volatility (Γ and ν), and time (tenor and time spreads).
- On the map, the sign of Δ follows direction and the signs of Γ and ν follow the volatility view; Γ and Θ almost always have opposite signs.
- “Long volatility” precisely means betting that realized volatility beats implied; for XYZ's at-the-money options the breakeven daily move is about $1.05.
- Short-dated options are mostly Γ and Θ, long-dated options mostly ν; differing views on near and far terms call for a time spread.
- The process: view → cell → structure → size by maximum loss; the most common mistake is mismatching the dimensions.

## @quiz
1. A position's signature is: Δ positive, Γ negative, Θ positive, ν negative. It is most likely:
   - [ ] a long call
   - [ ] a long straddle
   - [x] a cash-secured put
   - [ ] a protective put
   > Positive Δ = bullish; negative Γ and ν with positive Θ = selling volatility. That is the bullish × short-vol cell, home to cash-secured puts and covered calls. The long call and protective put are long volatility; the straddle's Δ is near zero.
2. Kai thinks XYZ will stay between 95 and 105 over the next 30 days and that 20% implied volatility is too high. Which cell fits best?
   - [ ] bullish × long vol
   - [ ] neutral × long vol
   - [x] neutral × short vol
   - [ ] bearish × term structure
   > No directional view (neutral) and a belief that realized volatility will come in below implied (short vol) — for example the 90/95/105/110 iron condor. The long straddle sits in the other row of the same column: it bets on a big move.
3. A call bought at 40% implied volatility before earnings loses 0.42 the next day, even though the stock rose 3%, because implied volatility fell to 20%. Which dimension was wrong?
   - [ ] Direction: the stock actually fell
   - [x] Volatility: the implied volatility paid was too high, and only a modest move was delivered
   - [ ] Time: the expiry was too short
   - [ ] Nothing — the option was mispriced
   > Direction (Δ) made money, but the loss through ν was larger. For a purely directional view, a low-ν spread fits better: in the same scenario the 100/105 bull call spread gains 0.70.
4. For a long-gamma position in XYZ's at-the-money options (σ = 20%), roughly how big a daily move is needed to offset theta?
   - [ ] about $0.20
   - [x] about $1.05
   - [ ] about $5.73
   - [ ] about $20
   > \(\Delta S^{*} \approx S\sigma/\sqrt{365} = 100 \times 0.2 / 19.1 \approx 1.05\). $5.73 is the one-standard-deviation move over 30 days, not per day.
5. A calendar spread (sell the 30-day 100 call, buy the 60-day 100 call) has negative Γ and positive ν. What view does it express?
   - [ ] The stock will rally hard
   - [ ] Both the near and the far term will be very volatile
   - [x] The stock stays near 100 in the near term while far-dated implied volatility holds or rises
   - [ ] Implied volatility will fall across the board
   > Selling the near month sells short-dated Γ (hoping for calm soon); buying the far month buys ν (hoping far-dated implied volatility doesn't fall). Γ and ν of opposite sign are the fingerprint of a time spread.
